The PhasorWaveSheet

A resonate-and-fire wave medium as a state-space model: derivation, dispersion theory, and discrete-SSM training — in PhasorNetworks.jl.

Scope & audience. This report is written for a reader fluent in hyperdimensional / vector-symbolic computing (HDC/VSA) and modern machine learning (state-space models, attention, mixture-of-experts) who wants to see how these pieces fit together into a single oscillatory substrate and how the mathematics is derived, not a tutorial on the individual concepts. Each new mechanism is introduced, its biological motivation stated where one exists, and the key steps are derived rather than asserted. Cross-references point to the source docs (rf_wave_network_implementation.md, wave_dispersion_derivation.md, wavesheet_experts_design.md) and to the implementation in src/wave.jl.

1. Motivation: waves as a computational medium

Two research programmes motivate this work. The first is oscillatory / phasor computing, in which information is carried by the phase of a common carrier oscillation rather than by a scalar activation. In the Fourier Holographic Reduced Representation (FHRR) branch of VSA [Plate 1995; Kleyko et al. 2022], a hypervector is a list of unit-magnitude complex numbers \(e^{i\varphi_k}\); the algebra of binding (element-wise complex multiply = phase addition), bundling (superposition), and similarity (cosine of phase differences) gives a compositional symbolic calculus that lives natively on oscillators. The second is the observation, now well supported experimentally, that the cortex is threaded by travelling waves that organise and route activity across the sheet [Muller et al. 2018].

The question this project asks is whether we can build a single differentiable substrate that (a) exhibits self-regulating, sparse wave dynamics, (b) runs efficiently on a GPU, (c) remains compatible with the phase-SSM view that makes phasor networks tractable to train, and (d) performs meaningful transformations by wave propagation itself rather than as a stack of discrete algebraic layers. The PhasorWaveSheet is that substrate: a two-dimensional recurrent lattice of resonate-and-fire neurons whose translation-invariant coupling turns the network into a wave medium, yet which reduces — exactly — to a diagonal state-space model that trains with ordinary reverse-mode AD.

Biological motivation

Cortical travelling waves arise generically from recurrent circuits with conduction delays and are implicated in gating, phase coding and sequence organisation [Muller et al. 2018]. The resonate-and-fire neuron [Izhikevich 2001] is the minimal spiking unit with an intrinsic oscillation, making it the natural building block for a phase-coding wave sheet.

2. The substrate: resonate-and-fire neurons in the phasor/SSM view

2.1 The resonate-and-fire unit

A resonate-and-fire (R&F) neuron carries a complex state \(z\in\mathbb{C}\) whose real and imaginary parts play the roles of a current-like and a voltage-like variable. Its sub-threshold dynamics are linear,

\[ \frac{dz}{dt} = k\,z + I(t), \qquad k=\lambda + i\omega, \]

where \(\omega\) is the intrinsic angular frequency and \(\lambda<0\) a decay rate. The homogeneous solution \(z(t)=z(0)\,e^{kt}\) is a decaying rotation: the phasor spins at \(\omega\) while its magnitude relaxes at rate \(\lambda\). Information is read off as the phase \(\theta = \arg z / \pi \in[-1,1]\) (units of \(\pi\)); a spike is emitted at the sub-cycle time encoded by that phase. This is the entire physical basis of the model.

2.2 The unified state-space model

Every layer in PhasorNetworks.jl — encoders, dense maps, convolutions, and the wave sheet — is defined by the same equation with a per-channel complex eigenvalue,

\[ \frac{dz_c}{dt} = k_c\,z_c + (W I(t))_c, \qquad k_c=\lambda_c + i\omega. \]

Discretising the leaky oscillator over one step \(\Delta t\) with a zero-order hold gives an exact linear recurrence — a diagonal state-space model (SSM) in the sense of S4/S5 [Gu et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2023]:

\[ z[n{+}1] = A\,z[n] + B\,u[n], \qquad A=e^{k\Delta t},\quad B=\frac{A-1}{k}, \]

whose impulse response (the discrete kernel) is \(K[n]=A^{n}B\). A layer can therefore be evaluated in several mathematically equivalent modes — a phase-domain causal convolution with \(K\), an FFT, or the continuous ODE solved by an adaptive integrator — and the same kernel \(K[n]=A^nB\) links them. This equivalence is what lets us train in the cheap, stable discrete mode and verify against the continuous one.

The per-channel-\(\omega\) rule. Because downstream layers compare phases by interference, every channel within a layer must rotate at the same carrier \(\omega\) (a single shared scalar, \(\omega=2\pi\)); otherwise the carrier desynchronises and HD/VSA invariance breaks. Cross-channel diversity lives entirely in \(\lambda\) (multi-timescale memory) and in the weights \(W\). The wave sheet respects this rule — indeed it depends on it (§3.1).

2.3 Binding as a phase operation

In FHRR, binding a transformation \(T\) onto a signal is the element-wise product \(z \mapsto z\odot \varphi_T\) with \(\varphi_T\) a unit phasor — i.e. phase addition \(\theta \mapsto \theta + \varphi_T\). Unbinding multiplies by the conjugate. This single fact recurs throughout the report: conduction delays (§3.1), the transformation "stamp" of the companion experts report, and the read-out phase shift (§4.5) are all instances of a unit-phasor multiply on the shared carrier.

3. From a feed-forward SSM to a recurrent wave sheet

In an ordinary phasor network the weight matrix \(W\) is a feed-forward inter-layer map. Make it a recurrent within-sheet coupling on a 2-D torus and the SSM becomes a wave medium. Each site \(x\) on an \(H\times W\) toroidal lattice carries an R&F state \(z_x\); the per-cycle update of a single neuron \(x\) sums the translation-invariant kernel, shifted to \(x\), against the whole sheet:

\[ z_x[n{+}1] = A\,z_x[n] + g\!\sum_{y} W(x-y)\,s_y[n] + \text{drive}_x[n]. \]

That neighbour sum is exactly the \(x\)-th entry of a spatial convolution, so stacking all sites gives the compact whole-sheet form (the one the spatial FFT diagonalises in §3.2 and the code evaluates as ifft(Ŵ .* fft(s))):

\[ z[n{+}1] = A\,z[n] + g\,(W \circledast s)[n] + \text{drive}[n], \qquad (W\circledast s)_x=\textstyle\sum_y W(x-y)s_y, \]

where every term is an \(H\times W\) field, \(\circledast\) is a (circular, toroidal) convolution with a translation-invariant kernel \(W\), \(g\) a global gain, and \(s\) the transmitted signal (below). The subscript \(x\) is what localises the convolution back to one neuron; without it, \(W\circledast s\) is the update for the entire sheet at once. The kernel is a difference-of-Gaussians (DoG): a short-range excitatory Gaussian minus a broader inhibitory one, i.e. a Mexican-hat with \(\int W \approx 0\) (spatial balance).

Biological motivation

DoG / Mexican-hat lateral connectivity — local excitation, surround inhibition — is the canonical cortical and retinal motif and the standard ingredient of continuous attractor and pattern-forming neural fields. The balance condition \(\int W\approx 0\) mirrors the excitatory/inhibitory balance that yields sparse, irregular activity [van Vreeswijk & Sompolinsky 1996; Brunel 2000], and tuning the network to marginal stability echoes the near-critical (branching-ratio \(\approx 1\)) regime associated with cortical avalanches [Beggs & Plenz 2003].

Three observations turn this recurrent SSM into something both tractable and GPU-native.

3.1 Insight 1 — conduction delays become complex weights

A physical wave sheet has conduction delays \(\tau_{xy}\) between sites; naively this is a delay-differential equation. But on a shared carrier \(\omega\), a delay \(\tau\) is exactly a phase rotation, \(s(t-\tau) = e^{-i\omega\tau}\,s(t)\) at the carrier. So the delayed coupling collapses to a complex weight \(W_{xy}=|W_{xy}|\,e^{-i\omega\tau_{xy}}\): the delay lives inside the linear complex SSM, with no DDE solver, no history buffer, and full differentiability. This is available only because of the per-channel-\(\omega\) discipline (§2.2) — the wave sheet is consistent with that rule, not an exception to it. (The identity is exact on the carrier; it is an approximation for broadband content.)

3.2 Insight 2 — translation invariance turns the coupled sheet into independent modes

The coupling links every neuron to a neighbourhood of others, so a single step is, in principle, a dense linear operator over all \(H\times W\) sites, and asking whether the sheet is stable means eigen-decomposing that \((HW)\times(HW)\) operator — hopeless to do literally, and step after step. What rescues us — and what makes the sheet a wave medium we can reason about in closed form — is a symmetry we have already built in: the coupling is translation-invariant.

That stencil is the difference-of-Gaussians of §3: a warm centre that excites nearby sites and a cooler surround that inhibits the ring around them — the Mexican-hat profile of Figure 2, whose spatial balance \(\int W\approx 0\) is what keeps the sheet near marginal stability.

A translation-invariant operator has a universal eigenbasis, independent of the particular kernel: the plane waves. A spatial mode is one such plane wave, \(e^{\,iq\cdot x}\) — a single global ripple washing across the entire sheet, its wavevector \(q=(q_1,q_2)\) fixing the ripple's wavelength and orientation (Figure 3). This is the crux to internalise: a mode is a spatial frequency, not a neuron-to-neuron distance. There are exactly \(H\times W\) of them because writing the field in the plane-wave basis (the 2-D DFT) is a change of coordinates, not a compression.

Now feed a single plane wave through the coupling. Because every neuron applies the same shifted stencil, the convolution returns the same plane wave, only rescaled by a complex number:

\[ W\circledast e^{\,iq\cdot x} \;=\; \hat W(q)\,e^{\,iq\cdot x}, \qquad \hat W(q)=\sum_r W(r)\,e^{-\,iq\cdot r}. \]

This is the convolution theorem, and it is the whole game: in the mode basis the coupling is diagonal. Each mode is an eigenvector; its eigenvalue \(\hat W(q)\) is nothing but the Fourier transform of the kernel-over-displacement — the one and only place the neuron-to-neuron distances enter. Modes (frequency) and the kernel's footprint (distance) are Fourier duals: both indexed by \(H\times W\), which is exactly why they are so easy to conflate.

Diagonal means the modes do not talk to each other. The single coupled sheet decouples into \(H\times W\) independent scalar recurrences, one per mode,

\[ \hat z_q[n{+}1] = M(q)\,\hat z_q[n] + \hat u_q[n], \qquad \boxed{\,M(q)=A + g\,\hat W(q)\,}. \]

Two payoffs follow. Analytically, each mode's fate is a single complex number \(M(q)\) — from which dispersion, group velocity and criticality all read off in closed form (§4). Computationally, all \(H\times W\) modes are advanced by two FFTs and an elementwise product per step — a batched cuFFT plus a pointwise multiply, which is the sheet's entire GPU story.

3.3 Insight 3 — the sheet is self-limiting

A purely linear recurrent sheet either decays or diverges. Two mechanisms keep it bounded and phase-meaningful. In the legacy phase-only mode (saturating=true) the state is projected to the unit circle each step, which discards magnitude. The default is spike transmission (transmit=:spike): each neuron transmits a fixed-magnitude event \(s_x = z_x/|z_x|\) (a unit phasor carrying its phase), so the coupling drive is hard-bounded, \(|\sum_y W_{xy}s_y|\le \sum_y|W_{xy}|\), giving a BIBO-stable leaky integrator without snapping the state — the magnitude \(|z|\) survives to encode local phase coherence. Sub-threshold neurons (\(|z|\approx 0\)) emit \(\approx 0\) through an \(\varepsilon\)-safe normalisation, a natural threshold.

Biological motivation

Spike transmission is the more faithful abstraction: neurons communicate through fixed-size action potentials whose timing (here, phase) carries the message, not through graded membrane potentials broadcast to their neighbours. This is also what makes the model self-limiting for free, since a spike's magnitude is fixed by construction. The spike lattice is formally a Sakaguchi–Kuramoto system (§4.3).

4. Dispersion: analysing the sheet like a solid

Section 3 reduced the coupled sheet to \(H\times W\) independent scalar recurrences, one per spatial mode \(q\), each governed by a single complex number \(M(q)=A+g\hat W(q)\). That is exactly the situation a solid-state physicist is in when analysing lattice vibrations: the crystal's translational symmetry decouples the atoms into independent phonon modes, and one reads a mode's behaviour off a dispersion relation \(\omega(q)\). We do the same here. Substituting a Bloch plane wave \(\hat z_q e^{i q\cdot x}\) into the continuous sheet gives the per-mode law directly, and from it every propagation property — how fast a disturbance travels, which wavelengths grow, and whether the sheet is stable — follows in closed form.

Two transmission modes — the distinction this section turns on. The sheet's coupling can carry one of two things between neurons, and the choice is what separates exact from linearised everywhere below.

In short: the potential mode is the clean analytic object — a linear wave medium whose Bloch modes propagate with an exact dispersion, valid for any amplitude and any superposition. The spike mode is the biophysically faithful medium — neurons communicate by fixed action potentials whose timing is the phase — but being nonlinear it has no exact dispersion; §4.4 recovers a Bloch-mode band only by linearising about a phase-coherent reference state, with the coupling gain renormalised to \(g/r\) by the spike normalisation. The two are thus complementary, not interchangeable: potential gives the exact propagation law, spike gives its local, gain-adjusted counterpart in the regime where the neurons actually spike.

4.1 The exact (potential-mode) dispersion

In the linear (potential-transmission) sheet, mode \(q\) obeys the scalar ODE \(\dot{\hat z}_q = \kappa(q)\,\hat z_q\), whose complex rate is the dispersion relation

\[ \kappa(q) = k + g\,\hat W(q) = \underbrace{\big(\lambda + g\,\mathrm{Re}\,\hat W(q)\big)}_{\textstyle \Gamma(q)\ \text{(growth)}} \;+\; i\,\underbrace{\big(\omega + g\,\mathrm{Im}\,\hat W(q)\big)}_{\textstyle \Omega(q)\ \text{(band)}}. \]

Splitting \(\kappa\) into real and imaginary parts separates two physically distinct effects, because a mode evolves as \(\hat z_q(t)\propto e^{\Gamma(q)t}\,e^{i\Omega(q)t}\). The real part \(\Gamma(q)\) is a per-mode growth or decay rate: it is set by the DoG magnitude, and because a Mexican-hat kernel suppresses the uniform (\(q=0\)) mode while boosting an intermediate wavelength, \(\Gamma(q)\) is band-pass — it selects a preferred wavelength \(q^\ast\) (Fig. 5, left). The imaginary part \(\Omega(q)\) is the mode's oscillation frequency: it is the carrier \(\omega\) shifted by the conduction-delay phase, and its \(q\)-dependence is dispersion in the optical sense — different wavelengths rotate at different rates (Fig. 5, right).

The discrete per-step multiplier of §3.2 is the one-period exponential of this rate, \(M(q)=e^{\kappa(q)T}=A+g\hat W(q)\), so the discrete SSM and the continuous dispersion are two views of the same complex number.

4.2 Group velocity: how fast information travels

A single Fourier mode is a standing ripple; information travels as a localised wave packet, a narrow band of modes around some carrier \(q_0\). Two speeds then matter (Fig. 6). The individual crests move at the phase velocity \(v_p=-\Omega(q_0)/q_0\); the packet's envelope — where the energy and the signal are — moves at the group velocity

\[ v_g = -\,\frac{d\Omega}{dq}\bigg|_{q_0}, \]

the local slope of the dispersion curve of Fig. 5 (right). Because \(\Omega(q)\) is curved, \(v_g\) depends on \(q_0\): different wavelengths carry information at different speeds, so an extended pattern spreads as it propagates. The sign follows the sheet's \(e^{+i\Omega t}\) convention; a flat \(\Omega\) would give a non-dispersive medium in which all wavelengths keep step.

4.3 Criticality as a spectral radius

Stability is not something to discover empirically during training — it is an eigenvalue condition on the very same \(M(q)\). Because each mode is scaled by \(M(q)\) every cycle, the whole sheet's fate is governed by the spectral radius

\[ \rho \;=\; \max_q\,|M(q)| \;=\; \max_q\,|A+g\,\hat W(q)|. \]

If \(\rho<1\) every mode decays and activity extinguishes; if \(\rho>1\) the fastest-growing mode diverges; and \(\rho\approx 1\) is the marginal, near-critical regime in which the selected wavelength \(q^\ast\) propagates without dying or blowing up (Fig. 7). Sweeping the coupling gain \(g\) moves \(\rho\) smoothly through 1 at a well-defined \(g_{\text{crit}}\), so "tune the sheet to criticality" is a one-line bisection, not a heuristic. The dispersion() routine returns \(M(q)\), \(\hat W(q)\), \(\Gamma(q)\) and \(\rho\) directly.

Biological motivation

Cortex is thought to operate near a critical point, where neuronal avalanches follow power-law statistics and dynamic range is maximised [Beggs & Plenz 2003]. Here criticality is literally \(\rho\to 1\): the marginal spectral radius is the computational sweet spot between a silent (\(\rho<1\)) and a saturated (\(\rho>1\)) sheet.

4.4 Spike mode: the Kuramoto linearisation

Everything so far is exact for the linear (potential) sheet. With spike transmission the coupling acts on the unit-magnitude events \(s_x=e^{i\pi\theta_x}\), so the phases obey the dynamics of a lattice of coupled phase oscillators — formally a Sakaguchi–Kuramoto model [Kuramoto 1975; Sakaguchi & Kuramoto 1986], in which the conduction delay reappears as a coupling frustration \(\psi_m=-\omega|m|/c\) rather than as an explicit time lag (Fig. 8).

This is nonlinear (the emit step is a projection to the unit circle), so there is no exact dispersion. But linearising a small perturbation about a phase-coherent reference state of amplitude \(r=g\,\hat D(0)/|\lambda|\) yields a closed-form phase-mode band

\[ \nu(q') \;=\; |\lambda|\left(\frac{\hat D(q')}{\hat D(0)} - 1\right), \]

with \(\hat D\) the delay-dressed coupling transform and an effective gain \(g/r\). It plays the role of \(\Gamma(q)\) for the spike sheet, and matches direct simulation of the spike lattice to \(\sim 10^{-4}\). So the two modes are complementary: the potential sheet has an exact dispersion, the spike sheet an exact linearisation about coherence.

What "coherence" means here. The reference state is phase-locked, not uniform: every neuron rotates at the shared carrier \(\omega\) with steady relative phases, so the spatial pattern is a rigidly-rotating shape — for the sheet, a steady travelling wave with a constant phase gradient. How strongly the population is locked is the mean-field order parameter of magnitude \(r\) (large \(=\) strongly coherent, \(r\to 0 =\) scattered/incoherent); here \(r=g\,\hat D(0)/|\lambda|\) is the amplitude the locked state settles to as coupling balances decay. The linearisation holds only for small phase deviations \(\delta\theta\) about this locked pattern and while \(r\) stays bounded away from zero — an incoherent sheet has no mean field to perturb about, the effective gain \(g/r\) diverges, and the band is meaningless. So \(\nu(q')\) describes ripples riding on an organised wave, not a desynchronised sheet.

4.5 Read-out phase shift during propagation

The dispersion also answers a question one actually asks of a phasor network: as a wave travels from where it is stamped to where it is read, how does its phase shift? Driving the sheet at the carrier and reading a point source at separation \(d\) gives a Green's function whose argument is precisely that read-out phase shift,

\[ \Delta\phi(d) \;=\; \arg\!\Big[\,\textstyle\sum_q \dfrac{e^{iqd}}{-\big(\lambda + g\,\hat W(q)\big)}\Big]. \]

Near criticality the sum is dominated by the selected mode, so \(\Delta\phi(d)\approx q^\ast d\): the read-out phase advances linearly with distance at the dominant wavevector (Fig. 9). The sheet is thus a computable, distance-dependent phase-delay line — exact for the potential mode, and the \(g/r\) linearisation for the spike mode. This is the linearly-readable channel; the interference intensity lives in \(|z|^2\), a square law no linear read-out can recover (§8).

5. Training on the discrete SSM (not the ODE)

The sheet has two evaluation tiers. Tier 2 is the continuous ODE \(\dot z = kz + g(W\circledast s)\), integrated by an adaptive solver (Tsit5) with BacksolveAdjoint+ZygoteVJP for gradients. Tier 1 is the discrete per-cycle recurrence of §3, evaluated as a causal convolution / parallel scan. Although the ODE is the "ground-truth" continuous dynamics (the two agree to field-similarity 0.997–0.9997), it is not the trainer — the discrete SSM is, for two reasons.

  • Stability is a knob, not a gamble. Training near \(\rho=\max_q|M(q)|\lesssim 1\) bounds the forward and backward pass by construction (§4.2). The adjoint instead reconstructs the trajectory in reverse time and accrues reconstruction/stiffness error.
  • Parallelism. Being LTI and diagonal per mode, the linear sheet's whole time evolution is a causal convolution with the geometric kernel \(K_q[m]=M(q)^{m-1}\) (FFT in space and time) or an associative scan — \(O(\log L)\) depth instead of the ODE's inherently sequential stepping.

The closed form of the \(L\)-cycle rollout is a homogeneous term plus a causal time-convolution of the drive,

\[ \hat z_q[n] = M(q)^{n}\,\hat z_q[0] \;+\; \sum_{m=1}^{n} M(q)^{\,n-m}\,\hat{\text{drive}}_q[m], \]

implemented in _wave_rollout_scan (exposed as wave_simulate(...; mode=:scan)). The geometric kernel is built as \(M(q)^{m-1}=e^{(m-1)\log M(q)}\) rather than by cumprod, which keeps it differentiable (the cumprod dims-adjoint is unsupported) and is branch-cut-safe for integer exponents. The parallel forward matches the sequential Buffer loop to \(\sim 6\times10^{-8}\).

Phase-per-cycle, not sub-cycle potentials. One discrete step advances a whole carrier period (\(A=e^{k\Delta t}\), \(\Delta t=T\)); the state we track and train on is the per-cycle phasor, and a neuron's sub-cycle spike time is its phase — exactly as PhasorDense's Dirac encoding folds sub-cycle timing into a one-shot phase map. The trainable path therefore never integrates a potential at an arbitrary sub-cycle time; the ODE's fine \(dt\) is a verifier artifact. The only thing that genuinely needs sub-cycle resolution is a hard threshold-and-reset nonlinearity (a discontinuity at an arbitrary instant), which is scoped to the ODE demo mode.

That is the trainer for the linear (potential) sheet — one scan does the whole rollout. The spike sheet is nonlinear, so its parallel trainer needs one more idea, which is the subject of §6.

6. Computing the spike sheet in parallel: the DEQ fixed point

This section is the computational counterpart of §5 for the nonlinear spike mode — how to evaluate and train it in parallel, not new physics. Spike transmission inserts a per-cycle nonlinearity (the emit \(z\mapsto z/|z|\)), so the rollout is not a single linear scan. But it is still a phase-domain SSM, structurally identical to PhasorDense's — only recurrent rather than feed-forward. The key observation is that the state is linear given the spike train: with the transmitted spikes \(s[j]=\mathrm{emit}(z[j{-}1])\) held fixed,

\[ z[n] = A^{n}z[0] + \sum_{j\le n} A^{\,n-j}\big(g\,(W\circledast s[j{-}1]) + \text{drive}[j]\big), \]

a parallel causal_conv with the scalar transport \(A\). Call this map \(\text{linear\_response}(s)\): a bank of independent leaky integrators (transport \(A\), no state-coupling) driven by the fixed spike train's coupling \(g\,(W\circledast s)\) plus the external drive. It is not the potential sheet of §3–4: there the coupling feeds back on the state, giving the transition \(M(q)=A+g\hat W(q)\); here it enters as an input because \(s\) is held fixed, so the transport is the bare \(A\). (The potential sheet is the \(\mathrm{emit}=\text{id}\) special case: with \(s=z\) the coupling closes back onto the state and the transition becomes \(M\).) The only nonlinearity is re-emitting \(s=\mathrm{emit}(z)\), so spike mode is a fixed point

\[ s^\ast = \mathrm{emit}\big(\text{linear\_response}(s^\ast)\big), \]

solved by iterating parallel sweeps with a cheap pointwise emit between them (_wave_rollout_deq). The map is strictly causal, so \(n_{\text{sweeps}}=L\) reproduces the sequential rollout exactly, while fewer sweeps settle toward it. This is a deep-equilibrium model (DEQ [Bai et al. 2019]): the output is the fixed point of one map iterated to convergence, with gradients taken through the fixed point by the implicit-function theorem (constant memory) rather than through the unrolled sweeps. It reuses the repo's attractor and equilibrium-propagation machinery and, again, avoids the ODE adjoint. Two emissions are provided: the unit phasor \(e^{i\pi\theta}\), and the Dirac-consistent \(e^{k\,dt}\) with \(dt=T(0.5-\theta/2)\), which reuses PhasorDense's encoding core so the two layers share one phase-SSM formalism (they agree in phase, differing only in the sub-cycle leak magnitude).

8. Limitations & open questions

Each of these is a direct consequence of the design, stated plainly so the claims elsewhere are not over-read.

  • The readable channel is coherence-limited — reading phase needs a reference. The sheet carries two kinds of signal with different readout requirements. The intensity of interference (how strongly waves reinforce) is \(|z|^2=\mathrm{Re}^2+\mathrm{Im}^2\), a square law: a readout that is linear in \((\mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Im})\) cannot compute it, so extracting interference strength needs a nonlinear (square-law) detector. The phase is linearly readable, but phase is only defined relative to a reference — either a coherent carrier (all sites share \(\omega\), so relative phases are well-posed) or an explicit reference signal to unbind against. On an incoherent field the pooled phasor sums to \(\approx 0\) and there is no phase to read. (Exactly what the decode experiments show: a "blind" phase read works only on a coherent carrier, while a "differential" read against a bind-free reference is robust.) So this is wave physics, not a fixable artifact: to read phase you must supply a reference, to read intensity you must square.
  • "Criticality" here is the linear stability edge, not avalanche criticality. The criticality of §4.2 is the linear spectral-radius edge \(\rho=\max_q|M(q)|\to 1\) — the marginal point between a decaying and a diverging linear sheet, and the point we tune and train at. That is not the self-organised, power-law avalanche criticality of cortex [Beggs & Plenz 2003], which is a nonlinear cascade phenomenon requiring a hard threshold-and-reset (a neuron fires and resets, seeding a branching cascade). That discontinuity lives only in the ODE demo mode, not in the smooth, trainable discrete path — so we may sit at the linear edge but should not claim genuine avalanche statistics. Relatedly, the isotropic sheet supports traveling waves (phase fronts) but not a self-sustaining traveling bump — a localised amplitude packet that moves while holding together, as in continuous-attractor networks. A symmetric, isotropic kernel has no preferred direction, so a symmetric bump merely spreads; making one travel requires breaking the symmetry (anisotropic coupling or adaptation), which the shipped isotropic sheet lacks.
  • The delay-as-phase trick is narrowband, and the dispersion needs the symmetry. Two consequences of the modelling choices. First, turning a conduction delay \(\tau\) into a phase factor \(e^{-i\omega\tau}\) (§3.1) is exact only at the carrier \(\omega\): a real signal occupies a band around \(\omega\), and a true delay acts as \(e^{-i\Omega\tau}\) frequency-by-frequency, so collapsing it to the single carrier value is excellent for narrowband content but only approximate as the bandwidth grows. Second, the entire closed-form dispersion of §4 rests on translation invariance — that is what lets the FFT diagonalise the coupling into \(M(q)\). The moment the connectivity is made patchy or spatially-varying (non-translation-invariant) — which is precisely what selective computation demands (§10) — the operator is no longer a convolution, does not diagonalise under the FFT, and the closed-form dispersion and spectral-radius criticality are lost. Such a sheet can still be simulated and trained; what is forfeited is the analytic handle, not the dynamics.

9. Reservoir computing: XOR in a bucket of spikes

The conclusion below argues the sheet is a medium, not a selective transformer. There is a classic way to compute with exactly such a medium: reservoir computing. A fixed, high-dimensional nonlinear dynamical system — the reservoir — projects an input into a rich transient state, and only a linear readout is trained [Jaeger 2001; Maass, Natschläger & Markram 2002]. The medium supplies the nonlinearity for free; the learning is a linear map on its state.

The canonical physical demonstration is Fernando & Sojakka's "Pattern Recognition in a Bucket" [2003]: ripples on a literal bucket of water, filmed by a camera, compute XOR — a function no linear classifier can compute on the raw inputs — because the water's wave interference is the nonlinear expansion and the camera's brightness (an intensity) is read off by a simple linear classifier. Our sheet is the same experiment with spiking waves instead of water. We treat the R&F sheet as a fixed reservoir (its coupling is not trained): the two bits \(a,b\) are injected as phase-encoded drives at two sites (bit \(\mapsto\) phasor \(e^{i\pi\,\text{bit}}\)); the spiking waves propagate and interfere across the sheet; and a linear (ridge) readout on the interference intensity \(|z|^2\) computes XOR.

The controls isolate what is doing the work. XOR is not linearly separable in the input, so a linear readout on the raw bits saturates near 0.74 (three of four cases). Before the waves meet (\(L=1\)) the injected field is unit-magnitude everywhere and the two inputs never overlap, so there is no interference and nothing to read (0.49, chance). The separable feature appears only after propagation, and it lives specifically in the intensity: a linear readout on \(|z|^2\) reaches 100%, while a linear readout on the raw \((\mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Im})\) field manages only 0.54. That gap is the square-law readout limitation of §8 made concrete — encoding a bit as phase \(0\) or \(\pi\), equal bits interfere constructively and unequal bits destructively, so \(|z|^2\) carries the parity (Fig. 10) while the linear field does not.

This is genuine computation in the medium, in the reservoir sense: the sheet performs a fixed nonlinear expansion (wave interference) and the only trained parameters are the linear readout’s. It is also, deliberately, not selective — the sheet does the same homogeneous mixing for every input, and it is the readout that extracts the task. That is exactly the substrate's strength and its boundary, which the conclusion draws out next.

10. Directed transport: breaking isotropy

§8 and the reservoir study leave the sheet as a medium, not a selective transformer: the isotropic difference-of-Gaussians is reflection-symmetric, \(W(\mathbf d)=W(-\mathbf d)\), so \(\hat W(\mathbf q)\) is even, the band \(\Omega(\mathbf q)\) is even, and the group velocity \(v_g=-\partial_q\Omega\) is odd — every rightward mode has an equal leftward twin. A perturbation spreads symmetrically with zero net velocity: information cannot be handed from one region to the next, so a spatial sequence of processors is impossible. This section is the constructive follow-up — adding directed transport, and deriving what it costs.

10.1 The cure: advection breaks reflection symmetry

Net transport requires an odd term in the coupling. A real, antisymmetric stencil — advection — is the minimal one:

\[ \hat W_{\text{adv}}(\mathbf q) = -i\big(\beta_h\sin q_h + \beta_w\sin q_w\big),\qquad v_{\text{drift}} = g\,\boldsymbol\beta . \]

It is purely imaginary and odd in \(\mathbf q\), so it adds a uniform group velocity \(g\boldsymbol\beta\) to the whole band (a drifting packet) rather than merely relocating it — a directional delay or an offset kernel only shift \(\hat W(\mathbf q\!-\!\mathbf b)\) and leave the \(\pm q\) twins counter-propagating, so neither transports. Because the stencil is still a function of the displacement \(\mathbf d\) alone, the coupling stays translation-invariant and FFT-diagonal — the parallel-scan/DEQ training of §5–6 is untouched. This is coupling = :aniso in src/wave.jl (two extra scalars \(\beta_h,\beta_w\)); at \(\boldsymbol\beta=0\) it reduces exactly to :dog.

10.2 Two spreading mechanisms, and which one bites

Directed drift is necessary but not sufficient — a packet also spreads, by two distinct routes. (i) Gain narrowing: the band-pass gain \(\Gamma(\mathbf q)\) is peaked at \(q_*\), so a broadband packet is reshaped toward the \(q_*\) ring and delocalises (rate \(\sim g\,|\Gamma''(q_*)|\)). (ii) Group-velocity dispersion (GVD): curvature of the phase band, \(\beta_2=\Omega''(q_*)\), so components drift at different speeds. The diagnostics dispersion_diagnostics/zero_gvd_speed read \(\Gamma,\Omega,v_g,\beta_2\) straight off the coupling (potential map \(M=A+g\hat W\) and the spike Kuramoto generator \(\Lambda\)), and they localise the problem: GVD is curable — there is a zero-dispersion conduction speed (\(c\!\approx\!2\) px/period) where \(\beta_2(q_*)=0\) with the drift intact — whereas gain narrowing is the real obstruction (\(|\Gamma''(q_*)|\approx2.2\) there), which is exactly why a seeded bump reshapes into the band-pass pattern and stalls.

10.3 The low-dispersion regime

A sharp spatial-frequency filter is necessarily dispersive — the same gain curvature that makes the sheet selective spreads packets. The resolution is to run the sheet as a near-neutral advective medium: a gentle Mexican hat at marginality (\(\Gamma(q_*)\!\approx\!0\)), operated at the DC carrier \(q_*=0\), where GVD vanishes for free (\(g\beta\sin 0=0\)) and \(\beta\) is a dispersion-free drift knob. A search over \((\sigma_E,\sigma_I,B_I,g)\) with the diagnostics finds this point and flattens the gain \(\sim60\times\):

10.4 The drift–dispersion trade-off (an honest limit)

Pushing the drift exposes a fundamental tension. Drift is \(g\beta\), but both low dispersion and stability push \(g\to0\) (a central-difference advection is only the leading term of a true shift, and its imaginary contribution raises the spectral radius). So ballistic, coherent transport reaches only \(\sim2\) bands before the packet spreads: a diffusive sheet cannot be at once a sharp spatial filter and a deep conveyor. The way out is to stop using a diffusive coupling for transport and use a shift (§10.5).

10.5 The shift conveyor: dispersion-free transport at any depth

A pure directed shift breaks the trade-off by construction. A translation by \(\mathbf s\) is the Fourier ramp

\[ \hat W_{\text{shift}}(\mathbf q) = e^{-i(s_h q_h + s_w q_w)},\qquad |\hat W|=1,\ \ \arg=-\mathbf s\!\cdot\!\mathbf q, \]

so the gain is flat (no narrowing), the phase is linear (constant group velocity \(\mathbf v_g=\mathbf s\)), and the GVD is exactly zero — a translation preserves the packet shape. Run with a strong leak (\(A=e^{kT}\!\approx\!0\)) so the shift dominates the self term, and the sheet becomes a ballistic delay-line: the diagnostics give \(v_g^*\!\approx\!s\), \(\beta_2^*\!\approx\!0\), \(|\Gamma''(q_*)|\!\approx\!0.02\) at marginality, and a seeded bump translates at \(s\) sites/period with its width fixed (spread \(1.38\!\to\!1.39\)) clear across the sheet. The price is honest: with \(A\approx0\) the sheet carries phase but has no resonant self-memory — it is a directed conveyor, not a diffusive wave. This is coupling = :shift (trainable shift vector \(s_h,s_w\)).

On this conveyor the wave-programmed pipeline runs at depth. A smooth payload injected at the top drifts through all \(E\) expert bands; each active expert stamps a VSA bind \(V_e\) as the packet passes; the composition \(\sum_e V_e\) is recovered by a differential unbind (bound run minus a transport-off control cancels the propagation phase):

11. Conclusion & commentary

The PhasorWaveSheet turns the phasor SSM into a genuine wave medium. Making the coupling a translation-invariant, delayed difference-of-Gaussians converts a stack of feed-forward layers into a single recurrent sheet on a torus, and three facts keep it both tractable and GPU-native: conduction delays become complex weights on the shared carrier (§3.1); the coupling diagonalises under the spatial FFT into \(H\times W\) independent Bloch modes with per-mode multiplier \(M(q)=A+g\hat W(q)\) (§3.2); and spike transmission makes the medium self-limiting (§3.3). From that one multiplier the entire physics reads off in closed form — growth band, oscillation band, group velocity, and a criticality knob \(\rho=\max_q|M(q)|\) (§4) — exactly for the linear (potential) sheet and, by linearising about a coherent state, approximately for the nonlinear (spike) sheet. And because the whole object is a diagonal SSM, it trains on the discrete recurrence — a parallel scan for the linear sheet (§5), a deep-equilibrium fixed point for the spike sheet (§6) — with no ODE adjoint.

What the sheet is — and, precisely, what it is not. All of the above says the sheet is an excellent medium: it transmits information as phase, propagates it with a speed and dispersion we can compute and tune, and can be biased to select and carry a particular eigenmode. It is worth being blunt about the other side. The sheet is not a selective medium for transforming information — it cannot apply one operation here and a different operation there, nor condition its action on the relative phases of particular neurons — and this is not incidental. It is the direct consequence of the very translation invariance that made the sheet analysable.

The argument is short. Translation invariance means the coupling depends only on displacement, \(W(x-y)\) — equivalently, the operator commutes with every spatial shift. A shift-invariant operator is a convolution, and a convolution applies the same rule at every location. That is exactly why it diagonalises in the plane-wave basis (§3.2) — and it is exactly why it cannot be spatially selective: an operation that treated one patch differently from another, or fired only when certain neurons' phases aligned, would depend on absolute position or on content, and would therefore break the symmetry. The single kernel carries only a handful of global knobs (the DoG widths and amplitudes, the gain, the decay); it can shape which spatial frequencies survive and how fast they travel, but it does the same thing to every site. It is spectrally selective, not spatially or content selective.

Two clarifications keep this honest. First, the sheet does mix neighbouring phases — that is what propagation and interference are — so it certainly transforms the field; but it transforms it homogeneously, by one fixed rule, not selectively. Second, nonlinearity does not rescue selectivity: spike transmission adds a self-limiting, amplitude-gating nonlinearity, but it remains translation-invariant, hence still homogeneous. A content-conditioned, location-specific transformation needs more than nonlinearity — it needs the symmetry to be broken.

This is the crux, and it frames what comes next. There is a real tension between the two things one wants: the invariance that yields a closed-form dispersion and a cheap diagonal trainer is the same invariance that leaves the medium computationally inert as a selective transformer — even though, as a fixed reservoir, it already computes (§9). To compute selectively on the sheet one must deliberately break the translation symmetry — introduce spatially-localised, content-gated modules that read a patch, decide, and stamp a transformation back into the passing wave. That is exactly the subject of the companion extension (Sparse VSA experts on the PhasorWaveSheet), and the price of the symmetry-breaking is already written into the limitations above: patchy, gated connectivity forfeits the closed-form dispersion (§8). The wave sheet, in short, is the substrate — an analysable, tunable medium for moving phase around; turning it into a computer is the work of the overlays that break its symmetry where, and only where, selectivity is needed.
