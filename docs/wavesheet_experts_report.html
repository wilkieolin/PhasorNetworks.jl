Sparse VSA experts on the PhasorWaveSheet

A transformer-style extension: gated, patch-local expert modules that read, route, and re-bind information into a passing wave — trained on the discrete SSM.

This is the extension to the companion report The PhasorWaveSheet: derivation & implementation, which derives the wave substrate itself — its dispersion, criticality, and discrete-SSM training. Here we build on top of that substrate. Section references of the form "main report §N" point back to it. Same audience: fluent in HDC/VSA and modern ML (state-space models, attention, mixture-of-experts).

1. What the substrate gives us

The main report establishes the pieces this extension depends on. The PhasorWaveSheet is a translation-invariant recurrent lattice of resonate-and-fire neurons that, in the Fourier basis, decouples into \(H\times W\) independent scalar state-space recurrences \(\hat z_q[n{+}1]=M(q)\,\hat z_q[n]+\hat u_q[n]\) with per-mode multiplier \(M(q)=A+g\hat W(q)\) (main report §3–4). It trains entirely on the discrete SSM — a parallel scan for the linear sheet, a deep-equilibrium fixed point for the spike sheet — with no ODE adjoint (main report §5–6). Information rides the shared carrier as phase, and binding a transformation onto a signal is a unit-phasor multiply (phase addition). Those two facts — a selective diagonal SSM and phase-domain binding — are exactly what the expert module below is built from.

2. Sparse experts: read → gate → re-bind

The sheet propagates information; the natural next step is to make it transform information as waves pass through — sparsely and selectively. The design is a bank of experts, each owning a patch of the sheet, that (1) read their patch, (2) gate themselves on or off, and (3) re-bind their output into the wave. This reconciles three literatures inside one substrate.

Biological motivation

The columnar organisation of neocortex [Mountcastle 1997] — a large folded sheet of repeated, locally-computing columns — motivates a repeated, patch-local "expert" unit, and the travelling-wave phenomenology of cortex motivates coupling those units through a propagating field rather than point-to-point wiring. That is the whole of the biological claim we lean on: a local unit repeated across a sheet, communicating by waves.

2.1 The bind is a diagonal insert into the recurrence

What "the recurrence" is. Everything in this section modifies one object: the wavesheet's own per-cycle update from the main report. In the site basis, advancing the whole sheet by one carrier period \(T\) is

\[ z[n{+}1] \;=\; A\,z[n] \;+\; g\,(W \circledast s)[n] \;+\; u[n], \qquad A=e^{kT},\;\; k=\lambda+i\omega, \]

or, diagonalised by the spatial FFT, the \(H\times W\) decoupled scalar recurrences \(\hat z_q[n{+}1]=M(q)\,\hat z_q[n]+\hat u_q[n]\) with \(M(q)=A+g\hat W(q)\) (main report §3–4). This — not a fresh block — is what an expert splices into. Because we set \(\omega=2\pi/T\) so the carrier turns through exactly one revolution per step, \(e^{i\omega T}=1\) and the self-multiplier collapses to a pure per-cycle decay \(A=e^{\lambda T}\in\mathbb R\): between events a phase-per-cycle step advances no net angle on its own.

How the insert changes the formula. An expert owning patch \(P\) re-binds its output onto the sites of \(P\): \(z_P \mapsto (g\,\varphi_e)\odot z_P\), where \(\varphi_e=e^{i\pi\theta_e}\) is the expert's unit-phasor code (phase \(\theta_e\in[-1,1]\)) and \(g\) a gain. Collect it into a diagonal operator \(D_e=\mathrm{diag}(d_x)\) with \(d_x=g\varphi_e\) for \(x\in P\) and \(d_x=1\) elsewhere. Splicing \(D_e\) into the per-cycle transition left-multiplies the update on the patch,

\[ z[n{+}1] \;=\; D_e\big(A\,z[n]+g(W\circledast s)[n]+u[n]\big), \]

so that on a patch site the per-cycle multiplier changes

\[ A=e^{\lambda T} \quad\longrightarrow\quad (g\varphi_e)\,A \;=\; g\,e^{\lambda T}\,e^{i\pi\theta_e}: \]

the update acquires a fixed phase advance \(\pi\theta_e\) and a gain \(g\) on that patch, and is untouched off-patch. \(D_e\) is the same kind of object as \(A\) — a diagonal per-site multiplier — so the module never leaves the SSM algebra; it merely makes the transition selective, the generalisation Mamba [Gu & Dao 2023] makes to diagonal SSMs. (In the implementation the equivalent input-injection form \(u\mapsto u\odot\big(1+\sum_e \mathrm{mask}_e\, g_e(\varphi_e-1)\big)\) modulates the injected drive rather than the state — regime 1 of §2.3 — which keeps the whole augmented sheet one parallel scan; with \(g=1\) the patch factor is exactly \(\varphi_e\).)

Sub-cycle placement. "Sub-cycle placement" is the instant \(t_b\in[0,T]\) within a period at which the bind physically fires: the free field rotates for \(t_b\), the stamp lands, then the field rotates for the remainder \(T-t_b\). Because \(D_e\) (a diagonal phase-and-gain) commutes with the diagonal free propagator \(e^{kt}\), \(e^{k(T-t_b)}\,D_e\,e^{kt_b}=D_e\,e^{kT}=D_e A\) — the per-cycle operator is \(D_e A\) regardless of \(t_b\) (Figure 1). The exact moment inside the cycle is a gauge the per-cycle step absorbs, which is why one phase per neuron per cycle is a complete state and the sub-cycle potential is never required.

Are we introducing a new frequency? No — provided the bind stays an event. Frequency is phase advance per unit time, \(\omega=d\phi/dt\), a property of the generator \(k=\lambda+i\omega\); the bind does not touch \(k\). Applied as a sparse, gated stamp, \(D_e\) is a one-off phase offset: it re-labels the phasor's angle by \(\pi\theta_e\) and rescales it by \(g\), a jump in the state, leaving the slope \(d\phi/dt=\omega\) (and the decay \(\lambda\)) unchanged on either side of the event (Figure 2). The caveat sharpens the point. If the same bind were applied on every cycle, the constant per-cycle increment \(\pi\theta_e\) would accumulate linearly in time — a genuine frequency offset \(\delta\omega=\pi\theta_e/T\) — and the repeated gain would shift the decay to \(\lambda+\ln g/T\); the bind would fold into the eigenvalue and give that patch its own carrier, breaking the shared-\(\omega\) phase-locking the whole architecture rests on. Sparsity is therefore not merely an efficiency choice: keeping the bind occasional is what preserves it as a phase offset rather than a new frequency.

A basis conflict carried into §2.3. One structural subtlety follows the insert downstream: a spatially local bind is diagonal in the site basis, while transport \(M(q)\) is diagonal in the Fourier basis. They do not co-diagonalise, so a bind spliced into the state transition is not a scalar scan; the scan stays scalar only when the selectivity modulates the input injection (regime 1 below) or is spatially global.

2.2 Routing: sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts

A mixture of experts (MoE) replaces one monolithic module with a bank of \(E\) parallel experts and a small router that, for each input, scores the experts and lets only a few of them act. When the router keeps just the top-scoring expert (top-1) — or, more generally, the top \(k\) — the layer is sparsely gated [Shazeer et al. 2017]: most experts sit idle on any given input, so the layer's capacity (its total parameter count) can grow with \(E\) while the compute per input stays fixed at whatever the \(k\) active experts cost. Our router is top-1, and each expert owns a patch of the sheet, so here "which expert" means "which patch gets transformed."

Two refinements from recent large-scale MoE practice carry over, and it is worth spelling out what each buys. The first is fine-grained experts [DeepSeekMoE 2024]: instead of a few large experts, use many small ones and activate a subset. This is the source of what we earlier called combinatorial routing flexibility — with \(E\) experts and \(k\) active, the number of distinct active combinations the router can form is \(\binom{E}{k}\), which grows combinatorially as the experts are split finer. A fixed active-compute budget therefore buys far more distinct "mixtures," which both raises representational capacity and pressures each small expert toward a narrow, non-overlapping speciality rather than a blurry average. (Our prototype is top-1, where \(\binom{E}{1}=E\), so it inherits the specialisation pressure more than the literal combinatorics; the combinatorial pay-off is what a top-\(k\) extension would unlock.)

The second refinement is how the router is kept from collapsing. Left to itself, a router tends to favour a few experts, which then receive most of the gradient and improve fastest, which makes the router favour them still more — a rich-get-richer spiral that leaves the rest of the bank dead. The classical fix adds an auxiliary load-balancing loss to the training objective, rewarding uniform usage. It works, but it also feeds gradients into the router that serve balance rather than the task — "interference gradients" that pull against the loss you actually care about. The auxiliary-loss-free scheme [DeepSeek 2024] drops that extra loss term entirely. In its place sits a per-expert bias: one extra scalar \(b_e\) per expert, added to that expert's routing score just before the top-\(k\) selection. A small control loop nudges \(b_e\) up for under-used experts and down for over-used ones according to each expert's recent load — and crucially this nudge is applied outside gradient descent, so it changes which expert wins the selection without contributing any gradient to the loss. Load is balanced by a side-channel controller rather than a competing objective; that is precisely what "loss-free" means here.

One piece remains: the top-1 pick is a discrete \(\arg\max\), which has no useful gradient. A straight-through / Gumbel-softmax estimator [Jang et al. 2016] bridges it — a hard one-hot route in the forward pass, the smooth softmax gradient in the backward pass. Together these are moe_gate (routing plus the straight-through gate) and update_moe_bias (the out-of-graph balancing controller) in the implementation.

In symbols, each expert summarises its patch with a mask-pooled complex read, turns the read magnitude into a routing score, and those scores drive a straight-through top-1 selection whose load — not a loss — balances the experts:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_e &= \tfrac{1}{|P_e|}\!\sum_{x\in P_e} z_x, & \ell &= W_r\,|r| && \text{(read}\to\text{logits)}\\[2pt] \mathrm{sel} &= \mathrm{onehot}\!\big(\arg\max_e(\ell_e + b_e)\big), & \mathrm{soft} &= \mathrm{softmax}(\ell/\tau) && \text{(select}\;\cdot\;\text{soften)}\\[2pt] g &= \mathrm{sel} + \big(\mathrm{soft}-\mathrm{sg}(\mathrm{soft})\big), & b_e &\leftarrow b_e + \eta\,\mathrm{sign}\!\big(\tfrac1E-\bar\ell_e\big) && \text{(STE gate}\;\cdot\;\text{loss-free balance)} \end{aligned} \]

where \(P_e\) is expert \(e\)'s patch, \(\mathrm{sg}\) is the stop-gradient, and \(\bar\ell_e=\langle g_e\rangle\) its mean gate load over the batch. The two ideas above appear directly in this algebra. First, the straight-through estimator gives \(g=\mathrm{sel}\) (a hard one-hot route) in the forward pass but \(\partial g/\partial\ell=\partial\,\mathrm{soft}/\partial\ell\) in the backward pass — a smooth gradient through a discrete choice. Second, the balancing bias \(b_e\) enters only the \(\arg\max\) that picks the expert, never the \(\mathrm{soft}\) whose gradient trains the router; balancing the load therefore never injects interference gradients into the task loss, and the bias update runs entirely outside the gradient tape.

2.3 Two routing regimes

A selective SSM stays parallel-scan-trainable if and only if its coefficients can be computed before the scan. Whether the gate reads the fixed input or the evolving state decides which side of that line an expert falls on.

Regime 1 — input-conditioned. The gate and bind read the input drive \(u\), so the modulated drive is a pure function of the input and can be formed before any propagation:

\[ u'[n] \;=\; u[n]\odot\Big(1+\textstyle\sum_e \mathrm{mask}_e\,g_e(\varphi_e-1)\Big),\qquad z \;=\; \mathrm{scan}_M\big(u'\big). \]

The gate coefficients \(g_e\) never depend on \(z\), so nothing serialises: the whole augmented sheet is one parallel scan (or, in spike mode, one DEQ sweep-set). This is the path the WaveExpertSheet prototype trains.

Regime 2 — state-conditioned. Here the gate reads the live wave — the literal "modify a wave as it passes by" — so the coefficients depend on the state and the single global scan breaks. We recover parallelism with event-based operator splitting: partition the \(L\) cycles into chunks of length \(\ell_c\), run the linear sheet as one parallel scan within a chunk, and apply the read→gate→bind event only at the \(\lceil L/\ell_c\rceil-1\) chunk boundaries:

\[ \tilde z \;=\; D_e\!\big(g(z)\big)\,z \quad\text{(boundary event)},\qquad z' \;=\; \mathrm{scan}_M(\tilde z)\quad\text{(next chunk)}, \]

with the first chunk propagating the initial field unmodified — an event needs a wave to act on. Because linear transport composes across boundaries, \(M(q)^{\ell_1}M(q)^{\ell_2}=M(q)^{\ell_1+\ell_2}\), an identity event reproduces the full scan exactly; the only serial cost is the number of events, which is small precisely because the experts are sparse.

2.4 Recovering the bind: VSA unbinding and resonators

Because the stamp is a VSA bind, it is recoverable by unbinding — the same algebra that VSA uses to attach and detach transformations from a carrier [Plate 1995]. When several codes are superposed, a resonator network [Frady et al. 2020; Kent et al. 2020] factors the product by searching in superposition; resonators have been realised as multi-compartment spiking phasor neurons on neuromorphic hardware [Renner et al. 2024] — the same substrate as the sheet's spike mode — and FHRR has been merged with deep residual/attention networks [repo author 2022]. Related reconciliations of oscillatory computation with attention/gating include Kuramoto oscillatory neurons composed with attentive connectivity [AKOrN 2024], attention on cellular automata [ViTCA 2022], and travelling-wave RNNs that store and transform sequences [Keller & Welling 2023; Keller et al. 2024]. The toroidal re-entry of the wave has a 1-D analogue in recurrent-depth / looped transformers [Geiping et al. 2025].

3. Empirical results

3.1 The go/no-go: does discrete routing survive the wave substrate?

The central risk of the expert design is that a discrete straight-through gate, reading from and writing into the wave, collapses to one always-on expert. On a synthetic "which patch is coherent" routing task the router reaches 100% accuracy with per-expert load staying spread (load-entropy \(\approx 1.2\) of \(\ln 4 = 1.386\), all four experts used) — no collapse [demos/wave_experts_gonogo.jl]. The straight-through gate gradient stays informative through the discrete-SSM rollout.

3.2 Decode check: is the stamped bind recoverable?

The stamped bind is recoverable: unbinding the output against a bind-free reference recovers every expert's code, robustly and even under an incoherent carrier. A blind decode is coherence-limited (100% → 0% as the carrier decoheres) — the intrinsic square-law/coherence nature of the readable channel. The prototype's per-expert bind is a 1-D scalar phase, so codes on disjoint patches decode independently but cannot be superposed and factored at one site; genuine superposition capacity would require high-D per-site codes plus a resonator.

4. Open questions

  • Superposition capacity. The prototype's scalar-per-expert bind has none; high-D per-site codes + a resonator are the path to carrying multiple bound transforms per wave, and the ceiling vs. hypervector dimension is unmeasured.
  • Scaling. The go/no-go and FashionMNIST runs are small and single-seed; scaling the expert bank, running on the spike-DEQ substrate, and larger real tasks remain to test.
  • State-conditioned routing at depth. The event-split keeps the state-conditioned path parallel within chunks; how gate quality and stability behave as chunks shrink (finer state feedback) is open.
  • Load signal on a torus. The DeepSeek per-expert bias balances token-style load; the right signal for spatially-localised patch occupancy on a 2-D torus is not yet characterised.
